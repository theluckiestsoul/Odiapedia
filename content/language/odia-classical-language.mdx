**Odia** (ଓଡ଼ିଆ) is one of the **classical languages of India**, a status the Government of India gives to languages with a long, well-documented and independent literary tradition. A Linguistic Experts' Committee recommended Odia on **23 July 2013**, and the Union Cabinet approved the status in **February 2014**. Odia was the **sixth** language to be recognised, and the first modern Indo-Aryan language to join Sanskrit on the list.

## When did Odia become a classical language?

The process ran over about a year:

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 23 July 2013 | The Linguistic Experts' Committee, meeting under the Ministry of Culture's scheme, recommended classical status for Odia |
| 14 August 2013 | The Ministry of Culture announced the recommendation in a Press Information Bureau release |
| 20 February 2014 | The Union Cabinet approved classical language status for Odia |
| 1 March 2014 | Date recorded for Odia in the Ministry of Culture's 2024 list of classical languages |

**Sources differ on the exact date.** Most accounts, including news reports at the time, use the Cabinet decision of 20 February 2014. The Ministry of Culture's press release of 3 October 2024 lists Odia against 1 March 2014, without explaining the difference. Both dates fall in early 2014; Odiapedia gives the Cabinet decision as the main date and notes the Ministry's date alongside it.

## Which languages are classical in India?

| Order | Language | Date in Ministry of Culture list |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tamil | 12 October 2004 |
| 2 | Sanskrit | 25 November 2005 |
| 3 | Telugu | 31 October 2008 |
| 4 | Kannada | 31 October 2008 |
| 5 | Malayalam | 8 August 2013 |
| 6 | **Odia** | 1 March 2014 |
| 7–11 | Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali | 3 October 2024 |

Dates follow the Press Information Bureau release of 3 October 2024. Some other sources give slightly different dates for earlier languages, for example the date of a Cabinet decision rather than of the formal notification.

With the five additions of **3 October 2024**, India has **11 classical languages**. Odia remained the only classical language from eastern India until Bengali and Assamese were added in 2024.

## What criteria did Odia have to meet?

When Odia was assessed in 2013, the committee applied the criteria set by the Government of India's 2004 resolution as later revised. In outline, a classical language needed:

1. **High antiquity** of its early texts or recorded history, over a period of 1,500 to 2,000 years.
2. **A body of ancient literature or texts** considered a valuable heritage by generations of speakers.
3. **An original literary tradition**, not borrowed from another speech community.
4. A classical language and literature that is **distinct from the modern form**, allowing for a discontinuity between the classical language and its later forms.

### How were the criteria changed in 2024?

On 25 July 2024 the Linguistic Experts' Committee revised the criteria. According to the Ministry of Culture, the new criteria are:

- high antiquity of early texts or recorded history over 1,500 to 2,000 years;
- a body of ancient literature or texts considered a heritage by generations of speakers;
- knowledge texts, especially prose in addition to poetry, along with epigraphic and inscriptional evidence;
- a classical language and literature that could be distinct from its current form, or discontinuous with later forms.

The requirement of an "original" literary tradition not borrowed from another community was dropped. The five 2024 languages were recognised under these revised criteria.

## What evidence supported Odia's claim?

Supporters of Odia's case pointed to a long written record in the region and a continuous literary tradition. The kinds of evidence usually cited include:

- **Inscriptions:** a long series of inscriptions from Odisha, from the Prakrit inscriptions in Brahmi script of the Ashokan and Kharavela periods to the Urajam inscription of 1051 CE, generally cited as the first written in Old Odia. See the [history of the Odia script](/language/odia-script-history).
- **Literature:** a body of medieval literature led by Sarala Das's Odia *Mahabharata* (15th century) and the works of the Panchasakha poets, plus a large stock of palm-leaf manuscripts. See the [milestones of Odia literature](/language/odia-literature-milestones).
- **Continuity:** a distinctive script and a linguistic identity that can be followed across many centuries.

Note that the oldest inscriptions from Odisha are in Prakrit, not Odia; the earliest inscription in recognisable Old Odia dates from the 11th century CE. Odia's case for antiquity therefore rests on the region's long written record and on the continuity between that record, Old Odia and the later literary language.

## What does classical status bring?

The 2013 announcement and the 2024 release describe these benefits:

- **Awards:** national and international awards for eminent scholars of classical Indian languages.
- **Centres of excellence:** the establishment of a centre of excellence for studies in the language.
- **University chairs:** the University Grants Commission is asked to create professional chairs in central universities.
- **Research and preservation:** support for documentation, digitisation, archiving, translation and publishing, with related employment in academia and research.

## Why does classical status matter for Odia?

For many Odia speakers, classical status was a matter of recognition. It placed Odia, a language once administered from Bengal, Bihar and Madras before Odisha became a separate province in 1936, alongside Tamil and Sanskrit as a language with a deep literary past. The status also brings funding and institutional attention to manuscripts, inscriptions and early texts.
