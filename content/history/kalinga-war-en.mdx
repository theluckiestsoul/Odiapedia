# The Kalinga War: Turning Tides of Humanity

The **Kalinga War** (c. 261 BCE) stands as one of the deadliest and most significant battles in world history. Fought between the **Maurya Empire** led by **Ashoka the Great** and the independent state of **Kalinga** (roughly present-day Odisha and northern Andhra Pradesh), it is traditionally believed to have been fought on the banks of the **Daya River**, near Dhauli.

## The Battle

Ashoka, aiming to expand his empire, invaded Kalinga, which was known for its maritime power and fierce independence. The Kalingans fought with remarkable valor, refusing to surrender their freedom.

*   **Casualties**: According to Ashoka's own Rock Edict XIII, 100,000 people were killed, 150,000 were deported, and many more died afterwards.
*   **The Aftermath**: According to popular tradition, the river Daya turned red with the blood of the fallen warriors.

## The Transformation of Ashoka

In Rock Edict XIII, Ashoka himself records his deep remorse over the suffering caused by the conquest of Kalinga. He turned away from conquest by violence (*Digvijaya*) and embraced conquest by righteousness (*Dharmavijaya*). Popular retellings often add a dramatic speech on the battlefield, but no such words appear in his inscriptions.

## Legacy: Dhauli Shanti Stupa

Ashoka became a devoted follower of **Buddhism** and dedicated the rest of his life to spreading the Dhamma, including non-violence (**Ahimsa**). The **Rock Edicts of Ashoka** at Dhauli stand today as a testament to this transformation. Interestingly, the edict describing the war (Rock Edict XIII) is not carved at Dhauli or Jaugada; instead, two Separate Edicts addressed to officials in Kalinga urge fair and humane rule. The **Shanti Stupa** (Peace Pagoda), built atop Dhauli hills in 1972 by Japanese and Kalinga Buddhist organisations, commemorates this shift from war to peace.

The Kalinga War is a rare instance in history where a victor renounced conquest by war after winning a war, choosing humanity over power.
