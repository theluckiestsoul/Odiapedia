**Nabakalebara** (ନବକଳେବର), also spelled **Navakalevara**, is the ritual in which the wooden images of **Jagannath**, **Balabhadra**, **Subhadra** and **Sudarshana** at the **Shree Jagannath Temple in Puri** are replaced with new ones. The name means "new body". It takes place only in a year when an **extra lunar month of Asadha** occurs, usually after 8, 11, 12 or 19 years, and the most recent Nabakalebara was in **2015**.

## What does Nabakalebara mean?

The word combines the Odia *naba* (new) and *kalebara* (body). Jagannath and his companions at Puri are carved from **wood**, unlike the stone or metal images of most Hindu temples. Wood decays, and the tradition holds that the deities periodically leave their old bodies and take on new ones, just as a soul takes a new body. Nabakalebara is therefore a renewal of the deities' bodies, not a replacement of the deities themselves.

## When is Nabakalebara held? The adhika Asadha rule

Hindu lunisolar calendars add an extra month (**adhika masa**, also called *Purushottama masa*) every few years to keep the lunar months in step with the solar year. According to the Shree Jagannath Temple Administration, when this extra month falls in **Asadha**, it becomes convenient to perform Nabakalebara: the additional time allows the trees to be found, the images carved and consecrated, and the deities dressed before the temple reopens for the [Rath Yatra](/culture/rath-yatra).

Because of this rule, Nabakalebara does not follow a fixed cycle. The temple administration describes intervals of **8, 11, 12 and 19 years**.

| Year | Interval from previous |
|---|---|
| 1912 | — |
| 1931 | 19 years |
| 1950 | 19 years |
| 1969 | 19 years |
| 1977 | 8 years |
| 1996 | 19 years |
| 2015 | 19 years |

Years from 1912 onward follow the standard published list. Earlier occurrences are recorded in temple tradition but are less firmly documented.

## The stages of Nabakalebara

The Shree Jagannath Temple Administration describes the ceremony in twelve steps:

1. **Banajaga Yatra** (Vanajaga Yatra), the journey to search for the sacred trees
2. Identification of the divine trees (*daru*)
3. Building temporary huts near the trees
4. Vedic rituals and fire sacrifices at the tree site
5. Cutting and shaping of the sacred trees
6. Carrying the logs to Puri
7. Building the work sheds and halls inside the temple
8. Carving the new images
9. Brahma rituals (*Brahmostav leela*)
10. Further secret rites (*Golakh leela*)
11. Burial of the old images (*patali*)
12. Completion of the new images (*saptavarana*, the layered finishing)

### Banajaga Yatra: searching for the sacred trees

According to the temple administration, the Banajaga Yatra begins on the tenth day of the bright fortnight of **Chaitra**. A party of servitors, including the **Daitapatis**, the **Pati Mohapatra**, **Biswakarma** carpenters and Brahmin priests, sets out from Puri.

According to tradition, the search party prays at the **Mangala temple at Kakatpur**, and the goddess reveals in a dream where the sacred trees will be found. This dream guidance is a matter of faith, and it is central to how devotees understand the ritual.

### What makes a tree sacred?

The images are carved from **neem** trees, called **daru** or *daru brahma* when chosen. One tree is needed for each of the four deities. Each must meet traditional signs. The commonly listed signs include:

- natural marks resembling Vishnu's symbols, such as the conch and the wheel;
- a location near water, a cremation ground, an anthill or a snake's hole;
- no birds' nests in the tree;
- other trees around it and a Shiva shrine nearby.

Published accounts of these signs vary in their details, and the temple administration describes them only as the required daru characteristics.

### Brahma Paribartana: the transfer at midnight

The most secret stage is the transfer of the **Brahma** (the sacred inner substance, *brahma padartha*) from the old images into the new. According to the temple administration it takes place late at night on the **14th day of the dark fortnight of Asadha**. A 2015 report in Sambad English described how only the Pati Mohapatra and three senior Daitapati servitors (*badagrahis*) carry out the transfer in near-darkness, lit by a single lamp, and stated that the popular belief that they are blindfolded is a misconception.

### Burial of the old images

The old wooden bodies are then buried within the temple precinct at a place called **Koili Baikuntha**. The new images are completed and painted, and the deities appear before the public again as the festival season moves towards the Rath Yatra.

## Nabakalebara 2015

The 2015 Nabakalebara was the first in 19 years and the first of the 21st century.

| Stage | 2015 |
|---|---|
| Banajaga Yatra began | March 2015 |
| Brahma Paribartana | Mid-July 2015, at night, in the days before the Ratha Yatra |
| Ratha Yatra with the new deities | 18 July 2015 |
| Expected visitors | More than three million, according to estimates reported at the time |

The ceremony drew national attention, and in 2015 some servitors spoke publicly about details of rituals that had long been kept from outsiders.

## Why is Nabakalebara important?

- **Living tradition.** The periodic renewal of the principal images reflects the distinctive character of the Jagannath cult, with its wooden deities and hereditary servitor communities.
- **Role of the Daitapatis.** The Daitapati servitors take a leading part, from the search for the trees to the transfer of the Brahma.
- **Mass pilgrimage.** A Nabakalebara year draws exceptionally large crowds to Puri during the festival season, including the [Snana Yatra](/culture/snana-yatra) and the Rath Yatra.
