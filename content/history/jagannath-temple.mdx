# Jagannath Temple, Puri — The Sacred Abode

The **Jagannath Temple** (ଜଗନ୍ନାଥ ମନ୍ଦିର) in Puri is one of the most sacred Hindu temples in India. As one of the four **Char Dhams** (sacred pilgrimage sites), it attracts millions of devotees annually.

## The Divine Trinity

The temple houses three deities made of sacred Neem wood:

| Deity | Description | Color |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| **Lord Jagannath** | Lord of the Universe | Black |
| **Lord Balabhadra** | Elder brother | White |
| **Goddess Subhadra** | Sister | Yellow |

Their unique, abstract forms with large round eyes and stump-like arms are interpreted by some as representing the limits of human understanding of the divine.

## Temple History

### Construction Timeline
- **12th century CE** — Current temple built
- **King Anantavarman Chodaganga** — Initiated construction
- **His successors** — Completed the temple; sources differ on whether the final work should be credited to Anangabhima Deva II or Anangabhima Deva III, who dedicated the kingdom to Jagannath

### Ancient Origins
The worship of Jagannath predates the temple by centuries, with roots in tribal traditions merged with Hindu practices.

## Architecture

The temple complex covers **more than 37,000 square metres** (roughly 9 to 10 acres) and contains well over a hundred smaller temples and shrines. The main temple includes:

### The Four Structures

1. **Vimana** (Sanctum) — about 214 feet (65 m) tall, houses the deities
2. **Jagamohana** (Assembly Hall) — For darshan
3. **Natamandapa** (Dance Hall) — For ritual performances
4. **Bhogamandapa** (Offering Hall) — For food offerings

### The Nilachakra

The **Nila Chakra** (blue wheel) atop the temple:
- Made of Ashtadhatu (8 metals)
- About 3.5 m (11 ft) high, with a circumference of about 11 m (36 ft)
- A new flag is hoisted on it every day
- A popular belief holds that it appears to face the viewer from every direction

The Nila Chakra should not be confused with the **Sudarshana**, the fourth deity worshipped in the sanctum alongside the trinity.

## The Temple Kitchen

The temple kitchen is often described as one of the largest in the world, and on festival days it can feed very large numbers of devotees:

- Hundreds of cooks and assistants work in it
- 56 varieties of Mahaprasad (the Chhappan Bhog)
- All dishes are vegetarian, without onion or garlic
- Cooked in earthen pots over wood fire
- According to temple tradition, pots are stacked one above another and the top pot cooks first

## Sacred Rituals

### Daily Routine
Timings vary by day and season; the main rituals include:
- **Dwarapitha and Mangala Arati** — Opening of the doors in the early morning
- **Mailama** — Changing of clothes and cleaning
- **Abakasha** — Morning purificatory rites
- **Sahana Mela** — Public darshan
- **Pahuda** — Deities retire for the night, usually late at night

### Special Ceremonies
- **Snana Yatra** — Annual bathing
- **Rath Yatra** — Chariot festival
- **Nabakalebara** — Renewal of the wooden deities, held in years with an extra month of Ashadha (typically after 8, 12 or 19 years)

## Unique Traditions

### Equality Before Jagannath
- **No caste discrimination** in Mahaprasad
- Everyone eats together
- The Gajapati King of Puri sweeps the chariot floors during Rath Yatra (Chhera Pahanra)

### The Flag
By custom, a new flag is hoisted on the Nila Chakra every day by temple servitors who climb the tower. The **Patitapabana** ("saviour of the fallen") form of Jagannath is also associated with the temple, historically giving darshan to those not allowed inside.

### The Mystery of the Flag
A popular belief says the flag flies **opposite to the wind direction**; this is a widely repeated legend rather than a documented phenomenon.

## Restrictions

- Only Hindus allowed inside
- Photography prohibited inside
- Dress code enforced

## Visiting Information

- **Location** — Puri, Odisha
- **Open** — Generally from early morning until late night (around 5 AM to 10:30 PM); hours change on festival days, so check with the Shree Jagannath Temple Administration
- **Entry** — Free (for Hindus)
- **Best time** — October to March
