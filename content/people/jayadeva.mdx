**Jayadeva** (ଜୟଦେବ) was a 12th-century Sanskrit poet best known for the **Gita Govinda**, a lyrical poem on the love of Radha and Krishna that became one of the most influential devotional texts in India. Jayadeva is closely associated with the Jagannath Temple at Puri, where the Gita Govinda has been sung as part of the deity's ritual service for centuries, and with the classical arts of Odisha, especially Odissi music and dance.

## When did Jayadeva live?

Exact dates for Jayadeva are not known. Encyclopaedia Britannica describes him as flourishing in the 12th century CE, and many modern accounts give a birth year of about 1170 CE, with a death in the 13th century. These figures are scholarly estimates rather than recorded dates, and readers should treat any precise year with caution.

Little reliable biographical detail survives. A verse found in Gita Govinda manuscripts names the poet as the son of **Bhojadeva** and **Ramadevi**. Tradition holds that his wife was **Padmavati**; later legends describe her as a temple dancer, but early commentators do not support this, and it is best regarded as tradition.

## Where was Jayadeva born? The birthplace debate

In the Gita Govinda, Jayadeva identifies himself with a village called **Kindubilva** (also read Kendubilva). Three places with similar names have been proposed, and the question has been debated by scholars for more than a century.

### The Odisha position

Odisha tradition, and a substantial body of scholarship, identifies Kindubilva with **Kenduli Sasan**, a village in the Prachi river valley near Puri. Britannica gives Jayadeva's birthplace as "the village of Kenduli Sasan, Orissa (now Odisha), near the city of Puri." Arguments advanced in support of this view include:

- **Inscriptions:** an inscription at the Lingaraj Temple in Bhubaneswar refers to a Jayadeva associated with the Kurmapataka school of temple performers, and later inscriptions from the Madhukeswara (Mukhalingam) and Simhachalam temples are cited as linking Jayadeva with this tradition under the Eastern Ganga kings.
- **Geography in the poem:** proponents read a phrase in the poem as placing Kendubilva near the sea, which fits the Puri coast better than inland sites.
- **Music and performance:** the ragas and talas named in the text are argued to match the Odissi tradition, and the poem has an unbroken ritual use at the Jagannath Temple.
- **Medieval references:** several later texts from different regions describe Jayadeva as coming from Utkala (Odisha).

Historians such as Kedarnath Mahapatra and art historian Thomas Donaldson are among those cited in support of the Odisha identification.

### Other claims

Many Bengali sources hold that Jayadeva was born at **Jaydev Kenduli** in Birbhum district, West Bengal, and served as a court poet of **King Lakshmana Sena** of Bengal; this tradition gained wide currency through 19th-century biographies, and Jaydev Kenduli hosts a large annual fair in his name. A third, less prominent, claim places him at a Kenduli near Jhanjharpur in **Mithila**, Bihar. A 2024 essay in *The Statesman* argues that the Lakshmana Sena tradition is chronologically difficult and that recent research favours Odisha, while noting that the existence of several villages, and possibly several writers, with the same names has added to the confusion.

Odiapedia presents the Odisha identification as the position supported by Britannica and much recent scholarship, while recognising that the question is not universally settled.

## The Gita Govinda

The **Gita Govinda** (Song of Govinda) is a Sanskrit poem in **twelve chapters** containing **twenty-four songs**, called prabandhas and popularly known as **ashtapadis** because most are built from eight couplets. It follows Krishna, his beloved Radha and her companion through longing, jealousy, separation and reunion. Within devotional traditions the human love story is read as an allegory of the soul's love for the divine.

The work is notable for specifying a **raga** (melodic mode) and **tala** (rhythmic cycle) for each song, which made it a performance text as much as a literary one. It also includes the celebrated hymn to the ten avatars of Vishnu (Dashavatara stotra).

### Place in the Jagannath tradition

The Gita Govinda is sung as part of the ritual of Lord Jagannath at Puri, and it became a foundation of **Odissi music** and of the **Odissi dance** repertoire, in which ashtapadis are standard abhinaya (expressive) items. See also [Odissi dance](/culture/odissi-dance) and the [Jagannath Temple](/history/jagannath-temple).

## Legacy and influence

- **Across India:** the Gita Govinda shaped devotional poetry, music and painting far beyond Odisha, influencing Vaishnava traditions in Bengal, Assam, Kerala and Rajasthan. In Kerala its songs entered temple music (sopana sangeetham).
- **Sikh scripture:** two hymns attributed to Jayadeva are included in the **Guru Granth Sahib**.
- **Translations:** Sir William Jones published an English translation in 1792; later well-known versions include Edwin Arnold's *The Indian Song of Songs* (1875) and Barbara Stoler Miller's *Love Song of the Dark Lord* (1977).
- **In Odisha:** Kenduli Sasan is honoured locally as Jayadeva's birthplace, and the Gita Govinda remains a core text for Odissi musicians and dancers.
